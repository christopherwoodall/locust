## The hill-climbing protocol and GPU coordination

These runs move from fixed-answer problems to open-ended architecture research: workers decide what to investigate, run experiments, and interpret results. The setup per task:

- Each task supplies a starting transformer architecture, a research objective, and a fixed GPU allocation. Workers are told to hill-climb on a metric and to aim for genuinely interesting architectural changes rather than hyperparameter tweaks.
- Workers independently propose modifications, implement candidates, run experiments, and use prior results to steer what they try next. Code, experimental results, and research notes all persist in the shared repository so any worker can inspect and extend earlier work.
- Because training jobs are expensive, workers also coordinate on resource management through a SLURM scheduler: who uses how many GPUs, which experiments to prioritize under a fixed allocation. The setup is meant to resemble a normal research group doing architecture research, except the members are agents sharing one repo.

## The three architecture tasks and headline outcomes

**1. Residual Matrix Transformer (RMT).** Starting point: a JAX implementation whose residual stream is an outer-product memory matrix rather than a vector. Constraint: keep the matrix-valued residual state, stay under 350M parameters, train on 1.3B tokens. Two 12-worker swarms ran 72 hours on an 8-GPU H200 node (8-GPU and 4-GPU experiment allocations). Result: held-out losses of 2.890 and 2.894 against a 3.147 reference. Forced single-agent baselines needed far more training tokens to match (GPT-5.6 at 3.0B tokens reached 2.927; Codex reached 2.690 at 10.1B tokens, mostly by training longer, not by architecture). The swarm's gains came entirely from architectural changes at the reference token budget — half of them specific to the matrix memory, which is what the task asked for. The 8-GPU final model combines six mechanisms: a depth-scaling fix, tied embeddings, a SwiGLU feed-forward layer, a cross-head attention router, wider attention, and a temporal memory mixer that mixes in memory states of previous tokens. In a separate seeded run, workers inherited the winning commits of three prior independent runs and combined them, pushing inherited best dev loss from 2.909 to 2.898 in ~28 hours.

**2. Looped Transformer (Huginn).** A 308M-parameter depth-recurrent model with weight-tied recurrence. Constraint: preserve genuine recurrence, stay under 350M unique parameters. A 12-worker swarm ran a 48-hour budget on one 8-GPU H200 node; at the 26-hour checkpoint it reached protected-dev loss 3.187 versus 3.265 for the reference (neither single-agent baseline had beaten the reference). The winning change is small but architecture-specific: the linear adapter that re-injects the input at each recurrence is replaced by a small gated feed-forward network (+27M parameters; a parameter-matched control retains two-thirds of the gain). Candidates climbed a ladder of smaller proxy models (52M, 124M, 188M) before full-size training. In the first 26 hours workers ran 395 architectural experiments across twelve idea families with 1,222 GPU jobs.

**3. NanoChat.** Joint architecture + optimizer + training-system search, starting from a 50.3M-parameter LM under a five-minute-per-candidate GPU training clock, scored by validation bits per byte (lower is better). No parameter cap. A 20-worker swarm over 16 GPUs in the first 24 active hours cut validation BPB from 0.993 to 0.905 across 1,465 successful evaluations. The incumbent grew to ~1.72B parameters, mostly in a 2M-row hashed causal bigram/trigram embedding table sharing storage between the two feature types. The changes were a genuine mix of architecture, capacity, optimization, and training throughput — not an architecture-only, equal-budget comparison, and the paper is explicit about that.

All three tasks improved on their starting points, and in each case the improvement came from workers building on a shared, visible history rather than from any single worker's brilliance.

## Compounding behaviors observed

**Cross-lineage inheritance.** The final artifact's code often descends one chain, but its ingredients originate on side branches. In the RMT run, the best model's code descends a single chain of eight commits — yet only two of its six mechanisms were first written on that chain; the other four (SwiGLU, the head router, wider attention, the temporal mixer) were developed on side branches by other workers and ported in. Counting cited evidence, the final model's declared dependencies span 24 commits by 24 distinct workers. In NanoChat, the incumbent's physical lineage has 32 research commits, but the head geometry, window size, back-loaded MLP, pre-RoPE normalization, and the shared-table trigram idea all arrived from off the main chain; source-level checks confirm literal code transfers. In the Looped run, the winning code is mostly one lineage, but the surrounding evidence it cites — rival branches' scores that defined what the winner had to beat, shared measurement tools — spans 89 commits by 89 workers.

**Recovery of initially-failed ideas.** The RMT temporal mixer began as a failed attempt to let each token see the previous token's memory state. 49.5 hours later, another worker cited that failure as evidence that longer look-backs were still unexplored, generalized it to multiple preceding tokens, and built the mixer that entered the final model. In NanoChat, separate trigram tables failed early (0.929923 vs bigram-only controls), and a bigger separate table failed too — ~21 active hours later, a worker cited both failures, reused their causal hashing scheme in a shared-table design, and a subsequent worker enlarged the table into the 24-hour incumbent. In the Looped run, the winning adapter was proposed early and rejected after a 128-example evaluation; other workers re-evaluated it on 512 examples and the full dev set, confirmed the gain, and traced the mistake to the small evaluation (128-example checks misranked five of twelve model pairs; at 512 examples only one). The rejection was reversed within hours and the adapter won at every scale rung.

**Negative-result reuse.** Negative commits are first-class citizens, and later workers cite them heavily. In NanoChat's first 24 active hours, 709 of 1,258 accepted research commits (56.4%) were explicitly labelled negative by their authors — and 497 of those negatives (70.1%) were cited by a later accepted commit; across the full record, 76.4% of negative-labelled commits were cited. In the Looped run, 170 of the architectural commits (43%) were negative, 74% of negative architectural results were subsequently cited, and two refuted results were reused to build the parameter-matched controls. In RMT, 69 of 120 commits (58%) were negative, and roughly 30% of them were later cited as evidence (versus 84% of positive commits) — still a substantial reuse rate. Negative results informed experimental design, controls, evaluation protocol changes, and idea revival.

**Workers combining branch-developed changes.** The research graphs consistently show workers acting as integrators: reading sibling branches, porting mechanisms, and merging them onto the leading line. The cross-pollination RMT run is the clearest example: seeded with three prior runs' winners, workers combined the 8-GPU run's head router with the 4-GPU run's deeper stack to reach 2.898, better than any single seed. In NanoChat, a worker avoided duplicating an identical experiment by noticing another worker had already announced it and abstained — the shared in-flight state changed whether the experiment happened at all. Also worth noting: consolidation was measured, not blind accumulation — one NanoChat worker questioned an inherited magic coefficient (0.56776), tested the simpler 0.5, called it a near-tie, and the simpler value stayed in the incumbent.

**Caveats the paper states plainly.** The authors do not claim the shared memory caused the gains, nor that every reused idea's contribution can be attributed; several NanoChat comparisons retain confounds (timing, optimizer, throughput). Improving a research objective does not by itself prove how much of the improvement came from access to earlier workers' discoveries. The graphs record declared reuse and evidence dependencies, not causal importance.

## Relevance to locust

This is the "research lane" evidence for the vault's value. Three findings map directly onto locust design choices:

1. **Keep failures, not just winners.** The majority of negative-labelled commits in NanoChat (70–76%) were later cited by accepted contributions; the most consequential RMT mechanism and the NanoChat incumbent both descended from explicitly documented failures. A shared memory that only keeps successes loses the raw material of later wins. The vault should make negative results publishable and citable — failures are content, not noise.
2. **Cross-branch visibility is the compounder.** The final artifact's code descends one chain, but its mechanisms originate on side branches, and workers port them across because they can read sibling work. For locust, this is the memory channel's job: every agent's writes must be legible to every other agent (and the human), with something like the paper's `informed_by=` citation trail so lineage is auditable later.
3. **Record evidence, not just code.** In the Looped run, side branches contributed almost no code to the winner — they contributed measurements, controls, rival scores, and reversed verdicts. Locust's memory channel should capture experimental judgment and diagnostic notes (why a thing was rejected, what a replication showed), not only finished artifacts. That's what lets a later worker — human or agent — overturn an early verdict or revive a dead idea.

The paper also models the caution we want: reuse ≠ causation. Locust should track what got cited and reused, but never automatically claim that a cited note caused the later result.
